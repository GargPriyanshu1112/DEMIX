The idea behind the reverse process is to **learn a trajectory** (or trajectories) **from a random noise to an image that closely resembles those in the training data.**

To learn these reverse trajectories, we need a lot of **intermediate noisy images.** These images, **which form the individual steps of the forward trajectories** (from clean data to noise), are generated by apply forward diffusion. The model when being trained with these intermediate noisy images, will try to learn the reverse trajectories.

The **resulting reverse trajectories resemble a high-dimensional vector field** that implicitly describe how to navigate from any point in the space towards the data manifold. The **data manifold is the conceptual space where all valid and realistic image samples exist**. Finding it is equivalent to revealing the underlying structure of training images.

**Directly calculating every possible trajectories from the intermediate noisy images to a realistic image**(the data manifold)**is practically impossible.** Moreover, we **don't even know what the target (realistic) image looks like beforehand.**

This makes a **direct, explicit computation intractable.** We can however, **train a model to learn and approximate the complex denoising trajectories.**

### Loss Function Derivation:

The derivation begins with the log-likelihood of the observed data points.

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i}^{N} log_e p_\theta(x_{i,0})
$$

For a **single observed data point, *x******i,0*** **representing original (real) image**, its likelihood can be expressed by considering the joint probability of *xi,0* and all the unobserved intermediate diffusion steps, *xi,1*, *xi,2*, ..., *xi,T* where *T* is the total number of diffusion steps and *xi,T* is pure noise.

$$
= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i}^{N} log_e \int p_\theta(x_{i,0}, x_{i,1}, x_{i,2},..., x_{i,T}) \, dx_{i,1}dx_{i,2}...dx_{i,T}
$$

    or

$$
= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i}^{N} log_e \int p_\theta(x_{i,0:T}) \, dx_{i,1:T}
$$

This process is equivalent to integrating out all possible trajectories in the diffusion process that could have led to the observed data point.

Now, multiply and divide the expression by the forward diffusion process, *q*. We get:

$$
= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i}^{N} log_e \int p_\theta(x_{i,0:T}) \, \frac{q(x_{i,1:T}|x_0)}{q(x_{i,1:T}|x_0)} \, dx_{i,1:T}
$$

Given the expectation formula,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[f(x)] = \int p(x) f(x) \, dx
$$

We can reformulate the above term as an expectation.

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_i^{N} \log_e \mathbb{E}_{x_{i,1:T} \sim q(x_{i,1:T} \mid x_0)} \left[ \frac{p_\theta(x_{i,0:T})}{q(x_{i,1:T}|x_0)} \right]
$$

Since **logE[x] ≥ E[log(x)]** because of  **Jensen’s inequality**, and log is a concave function, Jensen’s inequality ensures that

$$
\geq \frac{1}{N} \sum_i^{N} \mathbb{E}_{x_{i,1:T} \sim q(x_{i,1:T} \mid x_0)} \left[ log_e \frac{p_\theta(x_{i,0:T})}{q(x_{i,1:T}|x_0)} \right]
$$

Maximizing this objective (**ELBO**) leads to the maximization of the log likelihood.

From this point forward, to avoid notational clutter, we'll simplify the terms by **focusing on a single observed data point, *x*,** and removing redundant notations.

**Note:**

* The emperical average (averaging over N data points) will be addressed at the end of the derivation.
* ***q(x1, x2 | x0) is equal to q(x2, x1 | x0)*** as the order of events, *x1, x2* in a joint probability does not matter. It is known as the **commutative property of conjunction (AND)** in probability.
* We know, ***p(a,b) = p(a) * p(b|a)***  [conditional probability]
  $$
  p\big(\underbrace{x_0}_{B}, \underbrace{x_1, x_2}_{A}\big) = p(x_1, x_2) \ * \ p(x_0 | x_1, x_2)
  $$

$$
p\big(\underbrace{x_1}_{B}, \underbrace{x_2}_{A}\big) = p(x_2) \ * \ p(x_1 | x_2)
$$

    From eq. 1 and eq. 2,

$$
p\big({x_0, x_1, x_2}) = p(x_0 | x_1, x_2) \ * \ p(x_1 | x_2) \ * \ p(x_2)
$$

Rearranging the formula by applying ***logA.B = logA + logB***, we get

$$

The denominator in the above expression, because of the Markov property and the commutative property of conjuction, can be represented as a joint probability shown below:

$$
q(x_{t}|x_{t-1}) \ q(x_{t-1}|x_0) \ q(x_0)

$$
= q(x_{t}|x_{t-1}) * q(x_{0},x_{t-1})

= q(x_{t}|x_{t-1},x_{0}) * q(x_{0},x_{t-1})

= q(x_{t-1},x_{t},x_{0})

$$
\frac{1} {q(x_{t}|x_{t-1})} = \frac{ q(x_{t-1}|x_0) \ q(x_0)} {q(x_{t-1},x_{t},x_{0})} = \frac{ q(x_{t-1}|x_0) \ q(x_0)} { q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_{0}) \ q(x_t|x_0) \ q(x_0)} = \frac{1} {q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)} \frac{q(x_{t-1}|x_0)} {q(x_t|x_0)}

$$
= E_q \left[ log_e p_{\theta}(x_T)  +  \sum_{t=2}^{T} log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})} {q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_0)} + \ \sum_{t=2}^{T} log_e \frac{q(x_{t-1}|x_0)} {q(x_t|x_0)} \ + log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1)} {q(x_1|x_0)} \right]
$$

The second summation can further be simplified, as many terms in the numerator cancel with those in the denominator.

$$
= E_q \left[ log_e p_{\theta}(x_T)  +  \sum_{t=2}^{T} log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})} {q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_0)} + \ log_e \frac{q(x_{1}|x_0)} {q(x_T|x_0)} \ + log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1)} {q(x_1|x_0)} \right]

By again applying the property, log(a.b) = log(a) + log(b) to the 1st, 3rd and 4th term, we get,

$$
= E_q \left[ log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_T)} {1} \frac{q(x_{1}|x_0)} {q(x_T|x_0)}  \frac{p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1)} {q(x_1|x_0)} + \sum_{t=2}^{T} log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})} {q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_0)} \right]

Apply property, log(a.b) = log(a) + log(b) to arrive at the expression similar to the one found in the DDPM paper.

$$
= E_q \left[ log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_T)} {q(x_T|x_0)} + \sum_{t=2}^{T} log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})} {q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_0)} + log_e(p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1) \right]
$$

There are a few key adjustments needed for this expression. First, the distribution at the last step can be simply approximated as a standard Gaussian prior. This is because the data x at the last step is so corrupted by the iterative forward diffusion process that it is essentially just random noise following a standard Gaussian distribution.

$$
p_{\theta}(x_T) \Rightarrow p(x_T)
$$

Next, we need to negate this entire expression, as the negative log likelihood is commonly used as a loss functon for optimization,

$$
= E_q \left[ -log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_T)} {q(x_T|x_0)} - \sum_{t=2}^{T} log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})} {q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_0)} - log_e(p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1) \right]
$$

Since summation and expectation are linear operators, we can express the equation as

$$
= E_q \left[ -log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_T)} {q(x_T|x_0)} \right] + \sum_{t=2}^{T} E_q \left[ -log_e \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})} {q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_0)} \right] - E_q \left[ log_e(p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1) \right]
$$

The 1st and 2nd terms can be converted into Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence forms.

$$
D_{KL}(q(x_T|x_0) \| p(x_T)) \ + \ \sum_{t=2}^{T} D_{KL}(q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)) \| p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_t) \ - E_q \left[ log_e(p_{\theta}(x_0|x_1) \right]
$$

Now let's expand to the entire dataset.

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i}^{N} D_{KL}(q(x_{i,T}|x_{i,0}) \| p(x_{i,T})) \ + \ \sum_{t=2}^{T} D_{KL}(q(x_{i,t-1}|x_{i,t},x_{i,0})) \| p_{\theta}(x_{i,t-1}|x_{i,t}) \

- E_{_{x_{i,1:T} \sim q(x_{i,1:T} \mid x_0)}} \left[ log_e(p_{\theta}(x_{i,0}|x_{i,1}) \right]
$$

In generative modelling, the **true data distribution, q(x0)**, is not available, but there are a finite dataset of N samples. The idea is to approximate q(x0) using samples, which is also known as the **Monte Carlo estimation** or emperical estimation.

So in practice, the following expresssion is accepted.

$$
E_{q(x_{o})} [...] ≈ \frac{1} {N} \sum_{i}^{N} [...]
$$

Therefore the final form becomes,

$$
E_{x_o \sim q(x_{o})} \left[ D_{KL}(q(x_{i,T}|x_{i,0}) \| p(x_{i,T})) \ + \ \sum_{t=2}^{T} D_{KL}(q(x_{i,t-1}|x_{i,t},x_{i,0})) \| p_{\theta}(x_{i,t-1}|x_{i,t}) \

- E_{_{x_{i,1:T} \sim q(x_{i,1:T} \mid x_0)}} \left[ log_e(p_{\theta}(x_{i,0}|x_{i,1}) \right] \right]
$$

As you can see, the ELBO objective function consists of KL terms and one reconstruction term (the third term). The authors of the DDPM paper found that since *x0* and *x1* are very similar, this third term contributes little to the overall training signal, so they drop it during training. In addition, the first KL term is also discarded because it does not involve any of the model parameters; thus, minimizing it has no effect on optimizing the model.

Finally, the second KL terms are the main learning signal in the DDPM paper. For each step *t*, it compares the true posterior *q* conditioned on *x0* with the learned reverse process *pθ.* These KL terms are differentiable and can be computed analytically. Basically, the model is trained to minimize this mismatch at each step.

One good aspect about this term is that it involves two quantities of the same form. This is simply requiring approximate denoising transition distribution to be very close to the ground truth denoising transition distribution conditioned on x0.
